SN 12.2 / SĀ 298 · Definitions

Study deeper · Pāli and Chinese texts · Definitions

Shared teaching

Both present the twelve-link framework. They share the six classes of consciousness and the six internal sense bases.

Different classifications

Ignorance
SN 12.2 defines ignorance through not knowing the Four Noble Truths. SĀ 298 gives a longer list, including action and results.
Name
SN 12.2: feeling, perception, intention, contact, attention. SĀ 298: the four non-material aggregates, including consciousness.
Feeling
SN 12.2: six classes according to the contact from which feeling arises. SĀ 298: pleasant, painful, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant.
Craving
SN 12.2: six classes according to their objects. SĀ 298: craving associated with sensuality, form, and formlessness. This differs from the sensuality/existence/nonexistence classification cited elsewhere on this page.

Why it matters

The framework is shared, but definitions require a named source. Our plain-language feeling gloss uses feeling tone; the Text layer also records SN 12.2’s six contact-based classes.

Passage locator: SĀ 298, the explanations beginning 無明, 名, 受, and 愛; SN 12.2, the corresponding definitions. Select the text’s title carefully: the linked page also includes EĀ 49.5.

SN 12.20 / SĀ 296 · Principle and phenomena

Study deeper · Pāli and Chinese texts · Principle and phenomena

Shared teaching

The texts distinguish conditionality from dependently arisen phenomena and describe the regularity discovered and taught by a Buddha.

Different presentation

SN 12.20 explicitly describes each conditioned link as impermanent and subject to cessation. SĀ 296 arranges its explanations of the principle and conditioned phenomena differently. The two passages should be read in their own order.

Why it matters

The twelve links are an application of conditionality. Anālayo’s discussion of SN 12.20 helps explain why shorter formulations also express dependent arising.

Compare SN 12.20 / SĀ 296 · Chinese and Pāli ↗

Passage locator: the opening distinction between 因緣法 and 緣生法 in SĀ 296; the opening definitions in SN 12.20. See also Anālayo, “Dependent Arising” (2020).

SN 12.67 / SĀ 288 · Mutual support

Study deeper · Pāli and Chinese texts · Mutual support

Shared teaching

Both accounts describe reciprocal support between consciousness and name-and-form. This relationship is explicit in the texts.

Different analogy

SN 12.67 uses two bundles of reeds leaning together. SĀ 288 describes three reeds standing through mutual support. The Chinese analogy does not, by itself, establish a third doctrinal factor.

Why it matters

Reciprocity has support in both witnesses. The relation between this account and the standard sequence remains a matter of interpretation. We preserve the analogy’s difference without ranking either version as automatically more authentic.

Compare SN 12.67 / SĀ 288 · Chinese and Pāli ↗

Passage locator: SĀ 288, the analogy beginning 譬如：三蘆; SN 12.67, the passage beginning Seyyathāpi … dve naḷakalāpiyo.

SN 12.15 / SĀ 301 · The middle teaching

Shared teaching

Both connect dependent arising with a teaching that avoids the extremes of existence and nonexistence.

Reading the witnesses

The parallel supports the central theme. Its wording and presentation should be consulted separately rather than combined into a single reconstructed quotation.

Why it matters

Our Middle Way note has a comparative foundation. Agreement between witnesses strengthens a reading without establishing the Buddha’s exact original words.

What do the arrows mean?

Study deeper · What the arrows mean

Scholarly explanation of early discourses

Read each arrow as “with this as a condition.” The diagram selects relationships for study; several conditions may be involved, and the spacing carries no temporal measure.

Bodhi explains that the discourses include mutual support and other forms of conditionality. His introduction discusses variants that connect the factors differently. The relationships are more complex than a row of independent objects knocking one another over.

The open ring preserves the sequence in SN 12.2. Neighboring highlights guide your reading while leaving other conditioning relationships outside this view.

Kalupahana’s philosophical account distinguishes conditionality from fatalism: the conditions matter. It should be read as an attributed interpretation, not as a resolution of every debate about causation or free will.

Bodhi, Connected Discourses (2000), vol. I, pp. 522–524; Kalupahana, Causality (1975), pp. 89–95. Compare SN 12.67 · ATI ↗ · SuttaCentral ↗.

Why is this called a middle teaching?

Study deeper · A middle teaching

Early discourse · SN 12.15

SN 12.15 presents dependent arising between the extremes “everything exists” and “everything does not exist.” It directs attention to arising and cessation rather than attachment to a position about an enduring self.

Here, “middle” names an understanding of experience through its conditions, rather than a compromise between two opinions.

Cessation concerns the ending of the conditions sustaining suffering, rather than the destruction of a permanent self.

Read SN 12.15 · ATI ↗ · SuttaCentral ↗ · Bodhi, Connected Discourses, vol. I, pp. 544–545; introduction, pp. 522–524. This is an introductory paraphrase, not an exhaustive interpretation.

Does experience require a permanent self?

Study deeper · Self and conditions

Early discourse · SN 12.12

In SN 12.12, questions such as “Who feels?” are redirected toward the conditions for feeling. The inquiry proceeds through conditions, without positing an unchanging owner behind the process.

A change in the question

From “Who is the permanent experiencer?” to “With what condition does feeling arise?”

The discourse identifies contact as the condition for feeling. Pain remains real, and conduct and care matter. The inquiry examines experience without assuming an enduring substance that owns it.

Read SN 12.12 · ATI ↗ · SuttaCentral ↗ · Bodhi, Connected Discourses, vol. I, pp. 541–542, and introduction, p. 524. The question shift above is an explanatory paraphrase.

How do the Four Noble Truths fit?

Study deeper · Four Noble Truths

Early-discourse framework · scholarly explanation

Bodhi connects the arising and cessation sequences with the second and third noble truths. The wider framework explains the purpose of studying these conditions.

1 · Suffering
What is to be understood.
2 · Its origin
The sustaining conditions investigated in the arising sequence.
3 · Its cessation
The ending of suffering through the ending of its sustaining conditions.
4 · The path
The practice cultivated toward cessation: the Noble Eightfold Path.

The Upanisā sequence describes conditions supporting liberation. It complements the Eightfold Path while retaining its own name and distinct list of factors.

Philosophy & historical questions

How does this relate to emptiness?

Study deeper · Emptiness

Madhyamaka · Nāgārjuna through Garfield

In Mūlamadhyamakakārikā 24.18–19, Nāgārjuna connects dependent arising, emptiness, and dependent designation. In Garfield’s interpretation, things exist conventionally and function through dependence, without an independent, intrinsic nature.

Dependence makes change and practice intelligible. If suffering possessed a fixed, independent nature, its cessation would be impossible. Emptiness names this absence of intrinsic nature, not a hidden substance or permanent foundation beneath appearances.

Chapter 26 returns explicitly to the twelve links and liberation. This is a later Madhyamaka philosophical treatment, not a substitute for the wording of SN 12.2.

Jay L. Garfield, The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way (1995), Chapter 24, especially verses 18–19 and their commentary; Chapter 26. Garfield’s preface identifies his Indo-Tibetan Prāsaṅgika interpretive standpoint. Other readings exist.

Was the formula a response to Vedic ideas?

Study deeper · Historical questions

Historical reconstruction · scholarly argument

Joanna Jurewicz proposes that the twelve-link formula reworks Vedic accounts of creation while rejecting their assumption of an underlying self. Her paper compares the sequence and its terminology with earlier Vedic material.

She offers a tentative reconstruction with a limited scope: some canonical formulations fall outside her treatment. Read it as a historical argument, distinct from an established definition or the Buddha’s own recorded explanation.

Richard Gombrich endorses her proposal in his second-edition introduction. This is an endorsement of her argument, rather than independent confirmation. His Chapter II also discusses conditioned consciousness and the teachings’ Brahmanical context.

Read with two questions

What evidence supports the historical connection? What remains an inference about the teaching’s original setting?

Jurewicz, “Playing with Fire,” Journal of the Pali Text Society 26 (2000), pp. 77–103. Gombrich, How Buddhism Began, 2nd edition, introduction and Chapter II.
